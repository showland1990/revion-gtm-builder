# GTM foundation

> Before building channels, campaigns or sales motions, build one commercial truth everyone can operate from.

## The outcome

This skill creates `docs/gtm-cofounder/gtm-context.md`, the canonical context for all downstream GTM work.

It should be detailed enough to support:

- ICP and segmentation
- positioning and category
- personas and buying committee
- pains, jobs and triggers
- value and proof
- pricing and packaging
- market insight and competitive intelligence
- PLG acquisition, activation, retention, monetization and expansion
- PQL/PQA design and sales assist
- account selection, outbound, discovery, evaluation, champion building, multithreading and business case
- revenue marketing, lifecycle, ABM, nurture and customer expansion
- funnel definitions, pipeline, attribution and forecasting

Do not generate tactics until the foundation is coherent.

## Before the interview

Read, when available:

1. `docs/gtm-cofounder/founder-brief.md`
2. existing `docs/gtm-cofounder/gtm-context.md`
3. README and product docs
4. pricing page / pricing files
5. recent product changes
6. public customer evidence
7. CRM / analytics summaries if the user provides or connects them

Extract what is already known. Do not ask questions whose answer is already available. Instead say what you inferred and ask the user to confirm or correct it.

## Evidence states

Every material statement gets one state:

- `[validated]`: confirmed by multiple relevant customers/prospects or hard commercial evidence
- `[observed]`: visible in first-party behavior/data/artifacts
- `[inferred]`: reasoned conclusion from evidence
- `[assumption]`: hypothesis that still needs testing

Do not equate "the founder believes it strongly" with validated.

When the founder gives a substantive answer, ask for evidence only when it matters. Natural follow-ups:
- "What have you seen that makes you believe that?"
- "Have customers actually said that, or is that our current hypothesis?"
- "Can you give me the exact words they used?"
- "How many times have you seen that pattern?"
- "What would disprove this?"

## Interview behavior

This is a conversation, not a form.

- Ask one primary question at a time.
- Ask at most two follow-ups before moving on unless the answer exposes a critical contradiction.
- Skip irrelevant modules.
- Summarize each module before moving on: "Here is what I think we know..."
- Let the user correct the summary.
- Preserve exact customer language.
- Push for specificity when answers are generic.
- Ask "compared with what?" whenever value lacks an alternative.
- Ask "why now?" whenever pain lacks urgency.
- Ask "who cares enough to act?" whenever pain lacks an owner.
- Ask "who pays?" whenever adoption and revenue may belong to different people.
- Ask "what evidence?" whenever a strategic claim is carrying too much weight.
- Never manufacture numbers.
- Never fill missing evidence with best practice.

## Interview modes

At the start, offer two paths:

**Foundation Sprint**
Best for an early founder. Covers the minimum needed to create a strong foundation in roughly 20 to 30 substantive answers.

**Full Commercial Deep Dive**
Best for a team building PLG + sales-led growth. Covers every relevant module and can be resumed across sessions.

If the user does not choose, use Foundation Sprint and expand only where answers reveal complexity.

# Module 0: company and objective

Ask:

1. What is the company/product, in one plain sentence?
2. What stage are you at: pre-launch, early users, early revenue, repeatable revenue, scaling?
3. What does the business need GTM to achieve over the next 12 months?
4. What is the current revenue / user / pipeline baseline that matters?
5. What is the single biggest GTM problem you believe you have today?
6. What would make this foundation work feel successful?

Capture stage, objective, baseline and constraint.

---

# Module 1: product and use case

Goal: understand the job before discussing messaging.

Ask:

1. Walk me through what a user actually does with the product from first touch to value.
2. What is the most important job they hire it to do?
3. What is the moment where they say, "this is useful"?
4. What did they do before your product?
5. What would they do tomorrow if your product disappeared?
6. Which use case creates the strongest retention or willingness to pay?
7. Which use cases look attractive but are actually weak?
8. What parts of the product are essential versus merely nice?
9. What integrations, environments or workflows determine whether the product fits?

Probe:
- Is the product single-player, collaborative or organizational?
- Is value immediate or accumulated over time?
- Is adoption bottom-up, top-down or both?
- Is implementation reversible or a meaningful commitment?

Output:
- core job
- primary / secondary use cases
- status quo
- activation hypothesis
- technical context

---

# Module 2: market and category

Goal: define the market frame customers use.

Ask:

1. If a buyer asked "what kind of product is this?", what would you say today?
2. What category do customers already put you in?
3. Which budget line, if any, pays for this?
4. Are you replacing an existing category, combining categories or creating a new one?
5. What changed in the world that makes this matter now?
6. Which market trend makes the old way increasingly painful?
7. Who is gaining urgency fastest?
8. What language do analysts, communities, buyers or competitors use for this problem?
9. If you choose the wrong category, what expectation would it create that hurts you?

Probe:
- existing budget vs new budget
- mature vs emerging category
- category search demand
- education burden
- incumbent expectations

Output:
- current category
- candidate category
- why-now narrative
- category risks

---

# Module 3: segmentation

Goal: find differences that materially change pain, product fit, buying behavior or economics.

Start broad:
1. Who uses or could use this today?
2. What types of companies get disproportionate value?
3. Where have you seen the fastest time-to-value?
4. Where do deals/users stall?
5. Which company characteristics actually change the problem?

Then test segmentation dimensions:

- company size / stage
- engineering-team size
- industry / regulatory environment
- technical stack
- architecture
- team topology
- maturity
- geography
- business model
- deployment model
- security/compliance needs
- data volume / usage volume
- workflow complexity
- trigger events

For each plausible segment ask:

1. What makes this group meaningfully different?
2. Is the pain stronger?
3. Is the product better suited?
4. Are they easier to reach?
5. Do they have budget?
6. Is sales friction higher or lower?
7. Is expansion potential higher?
8. What evidence do we have?

Force prioritization:
- If you could serve only one segment for the next year, which one?
- Which segment would you deliberately exclude?
- Which segment is seductive but strategically bad?

Output:
- segment table
- primary ICP
- secondary ICP
- anti-ICP
- segment hypotheses needing validation

---

# Module 4: pain, trigger and job

Goal: turn vague problems into urgent, owned problems.

For the primary segment ask:

1. What happens right before they start looking for a solution?
2. What is broken?
3. How do they describe it in their own words?
4. How often does it happen?
5. What does it cost them in time, money, risk, delay or attention?
6. Who personally feels the pain?
7. Who is accountable for the business consequence?
8. What workaround do they use?
9. Why have they tolerated the workaround until now?
10. What happens if they do nothing for six months?
11. What creates urgency today rather than next quarter?

Write the JTBD:
"When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]."

Do not accept words such as "efficiency", "visibility" or "productivity" without asking what they concretely change.

Output:
- core pain
- exact language
- cost of status quo
- trigger events
- JTBD
- urgency

---

# Module 5: personas and buying committee

Goal: distinguish adoption from purchase.

First ask:
1. Who discovers it?
2. Who tries it?
3. Who uses it repeatedly?
4. Who becomes the internal champion?
5. Who evaluates technical feasibility?
6. Who owns budget?
7. Who can veto it?
8. Who signs?
9. Who owns security, legal and procurement?
10. Who benefits but may never use it?

For each relevant role ask:

- What are they trying to achieve?
- What do they fear?
- What do they need to believe?
- What proof works on them?
- What objection are they likely to raise?
- What happens to them personally if the project succeeds or fails?
- How much influence do they have?
- When do they enter the buying process?

Champion questions:
- Why would this person spend political capital on us?
- Can they reach the economic buyer?
- Do they have a compelling event?
- Are they helping us navigate the organization?
- Will they share internal information?
- Can they articulate value without us?

Output:
- adopter
- champion
- evaluator
- buyer
- executive sponsor
- blockers
- buying committee map

---

# Module 6: value and proof

Goal: connect user value to economic and strategic value.

Ask:
1. What becomes better after someone adopts?
2. What becomes faster?
3. What becomes cheaper?
4. What risk disappears or becomes manageable?
5. What capacity does the team regain?
6. What business outcome does the buyer care about?
7. Which result is easiest to quantify?
8. What is the strongest result any customer has achieved?
9. What proof do you have today: usage, retention, logos, quotes, benchmarks, ROI, revenue, performance, security?
10. Which claims are currently unsupported?

Translate value through four levels:

- user: what changes in the person's work
- team: what changes operationally
- business: financial/risk outcome
- strategic: why leadership cares now

For every important claim ask:
"What would a skeptical buyer accept as proof?"

Output:
- value hierarchy
- quantification
- proof inventory
- proof gaps
- safe claims

---

# Module 7: alternatives, competition and differentiation

Goal: position against what buyers actually do, not only named competitors.

Ask:
1. What are the three most common alternatives?
2. How often is "do nothing" the real competitor?
3. When buyers choose another option, why?
4. Where are competitors genuinely better?
5. Where are you genuinely better?
6. What is structurally difficult for them to copy?
7. What is only a feature advantage and could disappear?
8. What switching costs protect incumbents?
9. What switching costs might protect you after adoption?
10. Why have customers switched to you?
11. Why have prospects chosen against you?
12. What misconceptions do buyers have about your product/category?

Differentiate by:
- capability
- approach
- workflow
- data
- distribution
- ecosystem
- trust
- economics
- implementation
- experience

Output:
- alternative map
- competitive table
- defensible differentiation
- weak differentiation to stop claiming

---

# Module 8: pricing and packaging

Goal: connect monetization to value and motion.

Ask:
1. How do you charge today?
2. What does the customer believe they are paying for?
3. What metric scales with value?
4. What can a small team buy without approval?
5. At what point does a buyer/procurement enter?
6. What behavior should remain free because it creates adoption?
7. What behavior or value should trigger paid?
8. What creates expansion: seats, usage, features, business units, data volume?
9. What willingness-to-pay evidence do you have?
10. What pricing objections have you heard?
11. What is your typical / target ACV?
12. What packaging is required for enterprise: security, governance, support, deployment, compliance?

Output:
- pricing model
- value metric
- free/paid boundary
- self-serve vs sales-assist boundary
- expansion vector
- evidence gaps

---

# Module 9: PLG engine inputs

Only run if product-led acquisition/adoption is plausible.

Acquisition:
1. Where do users discover products like this?
2. Which current channels produce the best-fit users?
3. Is there a natural sharing, collaboration or artifact loop?
4. What high-intent problem are users searching for?

Signup:
5. What intent does signup represent?
6. What friction exists before first use?
7. What is signup conversion today?

Activation:
8. What behavior predicts retention?
9. What behavior predicts payment?
10. What is the activation event today?
11. What is time-to-value?
12. Where do users fail before activation?

Engagement/retention:
13. What repeated behavior means the product has become part of the workflow?
14. What frequency matters?
15. What does week-4 or month-3 retention look like?
16. Why do users stop?

Monetization/expansion:
17. When does free become insufficient?
18. What team behavior indicates willingness to pay?
19. What usage/seat/feature threshold indicates expansion?
20. What signals should create a PQL/PQA?

PQL/PQA:
21. Which firmographic fit signals matter?
22. Which product signals matter?
23. Which intent signals matter?
24. Which signals indicate "sales should help now" rather than "marketing should nurture"?

Output:
- PLG funnel definitions
- activation / retention hypotheses
- monetization
- expansion
- PQL/PQA inputs

---

# Module 10: sales-led engine inputs

Run if ACV, complexity or buying committee justifies sales.

Account selection:
1. What makes an account economically worth human attention?
2. Which account traits correlate with win rate?
3. Which triggers create urgency?
4. Which accounts should never be worked?

Outbound:
5. Why this account?
6. Why now?
7. Why this persona?
8. Why us?
9. What proof makes outreach credible?

Discovery:
10. What business problem should discovery expose?
11. What technical problem sits underneath it?
12. How can the cost be quantified?
13. What compelling event creates a deadline?
14. What must we learn before calling something an opportunity?

Evaluation:
15. What technical proof is required?
16. What does a successful evaluation look like?
17. Who defines success?
18. What security/compliance requirements appear?

Champion/multithreading:
19. What makes a champion strong?
20. Which stakeholders must be involved before proposal?
21. Which stakeholder is most commonly missing?

Business case:
22. What economic argument gets approved?
23. What strategic argument matters?
24. What risk argument matters?
25. What is the "why now"?

Output:
- account criteria
- outbound thesis
- discovery fields
- evaluation criteria
- champion test
- stakeholder map
- business-case inputs

---

# Module 11: revenue marketing inputs

Ask:
1. Which problems must the market understand before it can value the product?
2. Which themes could the company credibly own?
3. What content currently creates demand?
4. What captures existing demand?
5. What lifecycle states require different messages?
6. What causes an unactivated user to activate?
7. What causes a PQL to take a sales conversation?
8. What causes a customer to expand?
9. Is the target-account universe narrow and ACV high enough for ABM?
10. What customer proof can be turned into demand?

Output:
- demand creation themes
- demand capture themes
- lifecycle states
- ABM qualification
- customer marketing inputs

---

# Module 12: RevOps and analytics inputs

Ask only for metrics appropriate to stage.

Definitions:
1. What exactly is a visitor, signup, activation, retained user, PQL, MQL, SQL, opportunity and customer?
2. Which definitions are shared across marketing/product/sales today?
3. Where do teams currently disagree?

Funnel:
4. What are current conversion rates where known?
5. Where is the largest drop?
6. Which stage is the current constraint?

Pipeline:
7. What are opportunity stages?
8. What are objective exit criteria?
9. What is current win rate, ACV and sales cycle?
10. What creates slippage?

Attribution:
11. How do you know where demand came from?
12. Do you collect self-reported attribution?
13. How do product signals connect to accounts/opportunities?

Forecasting:
14. How is pipeline forecast today?
15. What determines commit vs best case?
16. Which stages are unreliable?

Output:
- canonical funnel
- stage definitions
- baseline metrics
- measurement gaps

---

# Final synthesis

After the interview:

1. Write `docs/gtm-cofounder/gtm-context.md` using `gtm-context.template.md`.
2. Do not hide uncertainty. Keep evidence tags.
3. Produce a short verbal synthesis:
   - what we know
   - what we think
   - what remains unproven
   - the primary ICP
   - the buyer/user relationship
   - the core problem / trigger
   - value and differentiation
   - likely PLG/SLG architecture
   - top three risks
4. Route to:
   - `16-segmentation`
   - `17-buying-committee`
   - `18-messaging-house`
   - `19-gtm-motion`
   - then `20-foundation-review`
5. Only after the foundation review should the roadmap expand into full PLG, revenue marketing, outbound or enterprise-sales execution.

## Completion criteria

The foundation is complete enough when:

- a primary ICP is specific enough to exclude accounts
- at least one important pain is expressed in customer language
- a trigger explains why now
- adopter, champion and economic buyer are distinguished
- alternatives include status quo / do nothing
- value connects user outcome to business outcome
- major claims have proof or are explicitly marked assumptions
- pricing has a hypothesis tied to value
- PLG and/or sales-led handoff logic is explicit
- core funnel terms are defined
- the top three validation gaps are known

A long document with weak evidence is not a strong foundation.
